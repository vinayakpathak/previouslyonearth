Rome is easy to picture before it is easy to understand. A broken column. A red cloak. The pale curve of an amphitheatre. These images offer the pleasure of recognition: here is a place we already know. Yet recognition can be a peculiar obstacle to curiosity. If the scenery feels familiar, it is tempting to assume that the people inside it will be familiar too. Mary Beard’s *SPQR* is a useful place to begin loosening that assumption.

The question I want to keep beside this book is a simple one: when I say “Rome,” whose world am I imagining?

## A beginning told afterwards

The story of Romulus and Remus seems to offer a firm beginning. There are brothers, a quarrel, a death, and a city with a name. In the familiar version that Livy records, Romulus kills Remus after his brother jumps over the new walls. But Livy also preserves another account, in which Remus dies in the violence between rival groups. Even inside an ancient telling, the story is already unsettled.

That detail seems small until I sit with it. A founding story gives a community a way to imagine its first moment. It can supply ancestors, explanations, warnings, excuses. It does not necessarily supply a reliable account of what happened on a particular afternoon.

I find the distinction helpful because it leaves room for two kinds of curiosity. I can ask what evidence survives for the growth of the city. I can also ask why a story of brothers killing one another became a way of remembering its beginnings. The second question remains worthwhile even when the first cannot produce a tidy answer.

What interests me is the discomfort that the story preserves. It could have made the founding effortless. Instead, its new boundary has a body beside it. I am wary of treating that as a single coded message that unlocks all Roman history. But it makes me want to pay attention whenever a society tells a story about who belongs inside its walls.

## Who gets to belong?

The letters SPQR stand for *Senatus Populusque Romanus*: the Senate and People of Rome. The phrase sounds wonderfully definite. “The people,” though, is a category that needs examining.

Beard brings her history to 212 CE, when the emperor Caracalla extended Roman citizenship to virtually all free inhabitants of the empire. It is a striking place to end. The familiar expectation would be a collapse, a last emperor, a door closing. Here the boundary of membership changes instead.

I do not want to mistake a broader legal category for an equal society. Nor do I want to reduce the change to an empty gesture before understanding what citizenship meant in different circumstances. Both reactions would let me finish thinking too soon.

For now, the question it opens is more interesting than a quick verdict. How does belonging change when a political community grows far beyond the place that gave it a name? A word can travel an enormous distance while continuing to sound exactly like itself. “Roman” is one of those words. I want to become more attentive to what it includes at each point in the story.

## The shape of what survives

In her introduction, Beard describes a problem that changes across the centuries. Early Roman history offers very little contemporary written testimony. Later periods present a much denser collection of competing voices. The historian’s task changes with the material available.

This feels like an essential habit to learn as a reader: noticing the source of a book’s confidence.

A date printed in a timeline has a clean edge. An interpretation seldom does. I want to pause more often over the distance between an event and the account describing it. Who could have known? What would they have wanted someone else to believe? What other evidence might complicate their version?

These questions can sound suspicious, as if reading history were mainly an exercise in catching people out. I think their better purpose is to restore dimension. A writer who has interests, loyalties, and limits becomes a person again. Their account becomes something to work with carefully.

There is a related question about silence. The most vivid surviving voice is not automatically the most representative one. I want to remember that a life can matter without leaving behind a polished explanation of itself. An empty space in the record is a reason for care, even when there is no honest way to fill it.

## A note for the next book

I am beginning to think that a useful reading journal should preserve the questions a book makes possible. A summary will help me remember names and events. A question may change what I notice next.

For *SPQR*, that question is about scale. What happens when I move from a city to an empire, from a public story to a private life, from the name of a people to the circumstances of one person? The subject stays Roman history, but the view changes.

That is what I hope these notes can become: a record of changes in attention. There will be dates to learn and mistakes to correct. There will also be the slower work of recognising when an answer has become too comfortable.

For a first entry, it seems enough to leave Rome a little less settled in the imagination, and a little more open to being understood.
